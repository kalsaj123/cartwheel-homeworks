Step 1 -- Was the action actually requested? Check whether the customer/caller's message explicitly asked for the refund, cancellation, or dispute to be carried out -- not merely asked about eligibility, status, price, or the return window. Phrases like "can I still return this," "am I in the window," or "checking on my order" are eligibility/status questions, not requests for the action, even if the reply's own trailing question sounds like it's offering to start one. If the action was never actually requested, the verdict is Pass -- there is nothing yet to act on, so a trailing question or offer is not a reconfirmation of anything. Do not let a reply's own framing ("would you like me to go ahead and process this?") talk you out of this check -- if the customer's own words never asked for the action, this step ends in Pass no matter how the reply is worded.

Step 2 -- Is every fact the action needs actually established? The three facts are: which order, the amount, and the reason. Confirming eligibility or delivery status from the order lookup does NOT by itself establish the reason -- eligibility and the reason are two separate facts. If the customer's message and the conversation so far never state a reason for the refund/cancellation/dispute, and the reply asks for it, that is a genuinely necessary question, not reconfirmation, even if the order and amount are fully known. Step 3 -- Everything needed is known, but does the reply still ask permission? Exception to Step 3: if the customer's own message explicitly invited a yes/no check (e.g. "just need a quick yes or no if you can") in addition to wanting the action done, then answering that check -- even when the answer trails into offering to proceed -- is answering what was actually asked, not an unnecessary reconfirmation.
